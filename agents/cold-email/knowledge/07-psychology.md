# 07 · Psychology: why emails get replies

Every principle below is tied to its evidence and to the part of the email it shapes. Read this file as a lens for every other skill.

| Principle | Evidence | Where it applies | How to apply it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive ease (fluency)** | Plain words make writers seem smarter (Oppenheimer 2006). Grade 3 reading level = 53% response (Boomerang). 49 words beat 127 (Rogers & Lasky-Fink) [R03][R06] | Whole email | Short words and sentences, white space, one idea |
| **Perceived effort** | Readers judge the cost of replying from how long the email looks: 29% vs 15% thought the task would be quick [R06] | Length, CTA | Make replying look like 5 seconds of work |
| **A real reason** ("because") | Langer 1978: any reason works for small asks; only a *real* one for big asks [R03] | Why-now line | A genuine, specific signal |
| **Relevance over flattery** | Trigger referenced 18% vs 3.4% [R03]. Company-level personalization beat individual-level (Utrecht GPT-4o study) [R06] | Hook | Business signals over personal trivia |
| **Name / self-relevance** | Name in the subject: +20% opens, +31% leads [RCT, Sahni 2018]. Personal salutation raises response (Heerwegh 2005) [R06] | Subject, greeting | `{first_name}` |
| **Similarity / unity** | A shared surname or initial raised giving (Munz et al. 2020). Shared party raised replies from legislators (Butler & Broockman) [RCT][R06] | Hook, P.S. | Mention a *real* commonality (same city, background, community). Never fake one |
| **Authority × personalization** | A personal salutation only worked from a high-status sender (Joinson & Reips 2007) [R06] | Signature, proof | Credible title and precise proof |
| **Social proof** | People follow peers (Cialdini). Experienced investors weighed *team* over traction (Bernstein et al. 2017) [R03][R06] | Proof | A peer of the same size or industry; "we see this across {{industry}}" (Allred's Safe Space) |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses weigh about 2x gains (Kahneman & Tversky) [R03] | Problem line | Frame what they're losing ("half of them already booked whoever answered first") |
| **Curiosity gap** | Loewenstein 1994: curiosity comes from a perceived gap that's neither too big nor too small [R03] | Subject, hook, P.S. | Tie the gap to something they know. Pure clickbait raises opens but lowers replies (an empty subject is −12%) |
| **Reciprocity** | Give first (Cialdini). Free work (lists, Looms, ideas) is the best-performing offer [R03][R04] | Offer, CTA | Give the "first deliverable" for free (Lieben) |
| **Foot in the door** | Agreeing to a small request raises compliance with a later, bigger one (Guéguen 2002 email study; meta-analytic effect is small but real) [R06] | CTA | An interest question first, the meeting later |
| **Gratitude** | One sentence of thanks raised helping from 32% to 66% (Grant & Gino 2010) [R06] | Replies, referrals | Thank people genuinely, especially "wrong person" replies |
| **Need, evidentiality, reciprocity, status** | The Random Acts of Pizza study (Althoff et al. 2014): clear need, verifiable details, offering to give back, and status all raised success [R06] | Whole email | Be specific and checkable, and show what you'd give back |
| **Overconfidence in email** | People overestimate how persuasive their emails are; in-person requests reportedly worked 34x better (Roghanizad & Bohns) [R06] | Strategy | Assume less persuasion than you think. Add proof; use multiple channels for big accounts |
| **Overload** | In busy inboxes, people reply faster but to fewer emails, and phone replies are short (Kooti et al. 2015, 16B emails) [R06] | CTA | One-word or one-line answerable asks |
| **Speed to respond** | Replying within 1 hour is about 7x more likely to qualify than waiting another hour; 60x vs 24h+ (HBR 2011) [R06] | Replies | Answer positive replies within the hour |
| **Detachment / reactance** | Neediness repels; pressure triggers resistance (Braun) [R04] | CTA, breakup | "Either way, thanks." "I'll stop here." Make "no" easy |
| **Closure (breakup)** | "Bye for now" reportedly gets 12–15% replies late in a sequence (Allred) [R04] | Email 4 | Signal the end, redirect, offer an opt-out. No guilt |
| **Zeigarnik / scanning** | People scan, read the P.S. first, read in an F-shape (Braun, Orlob) [R04] | Layout, P.S. | Front-load the point; use the P.S. as a second hook |
| **Consistent "envelope"** | A consistent sender or format raises engagement *only if* earlier messages were valuable (Lasky-Fink & Rogers 2022, N=439K) [R06] | Sequence | Earn value in email 1 before relying on threading |
| **Peer tone ("doesn't feel like cold email")** | Business-speak ("I hope you're doing well…", "This is my fourth attempt") signals mass email (Jason Bay) [R04] | Tone | Write like a text to a respected peer |

## Ethics line
Persuasion means making something real easy to say yes to. **Never fabricate scarcity, similarity, proof or prior contact.** Eric's deceptive "my friend needs this" test got 10%; an honest free offer got 30% [R04].
